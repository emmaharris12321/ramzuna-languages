Online lessons make it possible to connect a student in Gaza with a volunteer educator in Toronto — something that simply was not possible a generation ago. But learning through a screen also comes with its own challenges: unreliable internet, scheduling across time zones, and the temptation to skip a lesson when life gets busy.

## Set a Fixed Weekly Time

Students who treat their lesson like a fixed appointment, rather than something to schedule around, tend to stick with the programme longer. Put it in your calendar, at the same time each week if possible, so it becomes part of your routine rather than one more thing to remember.

## Have a Backup Plan for Connectivity

Internet interruptions happen. Agree with your educator ahead of time on a backup plan — switching to audio-only, rescheduling within the same week, or continuing by text message — so a bad connection does not turn into a missed lesson entirely.

## Keep a Simple Notebook

You do not need fancy apps. A single notebook where you jot down new words, corrected sentences, and questions for next time gives you something concrete to review between lessons and track your own progress over weeks and months.

## Tell Your Educator What Is Working

Volunteer educators want their lessons to actually help. If a particular exercise is not useful, or you would like more conversation practice and less grammar drilling, say so. The lessons are personalised to you, and that only works if you speak up.

## Celebrate Small Wins

Understanding a joke, reading a sentence without translating it in your head, holding a five-minute conversation without switching back to your first language — these moments matter. Progress in a language rarely feels dramatic day to day, but it adds up faster than most students expect.

<p style="text-align: justify;">
A topic sentence tells the reader what a body paragraph is about and how it supports your thesis. Write one at the start of every body paragraph, straight after your introduction.
</p>

## What a topic sentence does

<p style="text-align: justify;">
It is the first of the four elements in a body paragraph (topic sentence, evidence, analysis, link), and it has two jobs.
</p>

<ol style="text-align: justify;">
  <li><strong>Support your thesis.</strong> Whatever point your thesis makes, each topic sentence must back it up. Otherwise your essay drifts off topic.</li>
  <li><strong>Preview the paragraph.</strong> It describes what the paragraph is about, and every other sentence in the paragraph must support it, just as the topic sentence supports the thesis.</li>
</ol>

## Example: from thesis to topic sentences

<p style="text-align: justify;">
Suppose your thesis is: <em>Pizza is one of America’s favorite foods because it is delicious, convenient, and cheap.</em> The thesis is specific, so you can expect three topic sentences, one for each reason.
</p>

<p style="text-align: justify;">
Each topic sentence names one reason from the thesis and tells you what evidence and detail to expect in the rest of the paragraph.
</p>

## Quick checklist

<ul style="text-align: justify;">
  <li>Does it support my thesis?</li>
  <li>Does it name one clear idea?</li>
  <li>Does it show what the rest of the paragraph will cover?</li>
  <li>Can every other sentence in the paragraph support it?</li>
</ul>

## Common mistakes

<ul style="text-align: justify;">
  <li><strong>Off-thesis topic sentences.</strong> If a topic sentence does not connect back to your thesis, the paragraph takes your essay off topic.</li>
  <li><strong>Vague topic sentences.</strong> If the reader cannot tell what the paragraph will cover, rewrite it to name the specific idea.</li>
  <li><strong>Too many ideas.</strong> One paragraph, one point. A second idea needs its own paragraph and topic sentence.</li>
</ul>

Every volunteer educator who joins Ramzunā Languages brings real skill and real care to their lessons. But even experienced teachers can fall into habits that quietly limit how much their students grow. Here are five of the most common ones we see — and what to do instead.

## 1. Talking More Than You Listen

It is easy for a lesson to turn into a monologue, especially when you are explaining a tricky grammar point. But language is built through use, not through listening to someone else use it. Aim for your student to be speaking for at least half of every lesson. If you notice you have been talking for several minutes straight, that is a good moment to ask a question and hand the conversation back.

## 2. Teaching Content, Not Context

Vocabulary lists and grammar drills matter, but they only stick when they are attached to something real. Instead of teaching the word "interview" in isolation, build a lesson around a mock job interview your student might actually face. Context turns information into something a student can actually use the next day.

## 3. Focusing on Grades Over Growth

Our students are not working toward a report card — they are working toward confidence, opportunity, and connection. A student who makes fewer mistakes but never speaks up is not necessarily doing better than one who talks constantly and gets things wrong. Notice and name progress in fluency and confidence, not only in accuracy.

## 4. Using One Method for Every Learner

What works brilliantly for one student can fall completely flat for another. Some learners thrive on structured grammar explanations; others learn best through stories, games, or open conversation. Stay curious about how your specific student learns, and be willing to change your approach mid-term if something is not landing.

## 5. Forgetting to Keep Learning

The best educators treat teaching as a two-way skill, not a fixed one. Ask your students what is working and what is not. Talk to other volunteer educators about what they try in their own lessons. Small adjustments, made consistently, are what separate a good teacher from a great one.

None of these mistakes make you a bad educator — they are simply easy traps that almost every teacher falls into at some point. Noticing them is most of the work. The rest is just staying curious, lesson after lesson.
